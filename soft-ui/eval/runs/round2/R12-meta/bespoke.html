Meta-analysis · 9 RCTs · CBT-I vs usual care · sleep onset latency

CBT-I shortens sleep onset by about 10 minutes, and the result holds without the high-bias trials

The random-effects pooled mean difference is −9.8 min (95% CI −12.4 to −7.2; p<0.001), with moderate certainty (GRADE). Every trial points the same way. Heterogeneity is modest (I² 38%). With only the five low-bias trials, the effect is −8.6 min (−10.6 to −6.6): smaller, but still clear.

Do the high-bias studies drive the result?

No. They report the largest effects, but they are small and carry only 7.7% of the weight.

Restricting to low-bias trials moves the estimate by 1.2 min toward zero (−9.8 → −8.6). The two confidence intervals overlap and both exclude zero. Report −8.6 as the conservative figure.

Smaller trials report larger effects

Effect against sample size. The two high-bias trials (n = 48 and 36) give the strongest effects; trials above n = 100 cluster between −8 and −11 min. This pattern fits small-study effects, but 9 trials are too few for a formal funnel-plot test.

How the outcome is distributed in the largest trial

Hale 2022, n = 300 (150 per arm), 27.4% of the pooled weight. Raw sleep-onset minutes for each participant. CBT-I bars point up, usual care bars point down; lines mark the medians.

The whole CBT-I distribution shifts left, so the mean effect is not the product of a few responders. Both arms are roughly symmetric with a mild right tail, so a mean difference is a fair summary. The raw difference in means (−8.4 min) is a little larger than the −7.8 min that enters the meta-analysis; check whether the trial reported an adjusted estimate.

Draft text for the discussion

Across nine randomised trials, CBT-I reduced sleep onset latency by 9.8 minutes compared with usual care (95% CI 7.2 to 12.4; random effects, REML), and the certainty of the evidence was moderate (GRADE). All trials favoured CBT-I, and heterogeneity was modest (I² = 38%, τ² = 4.1, Q-test p = 0.12).

The two trials at high risk of bias reported the largest effects (−21.0 and −25.5 minutes) but contributed only 7.7% of the weight. A sensitivity analysis restricted to the five trials at low risk of bias gave a smaller but consistent effect of −8.6 minutes (95% CI −10.6 to −6.6). Smaller trials tended to report larger effects, which suggests possible small-study effects; too few trials were available to test this formally.

In the largest trial (n = 300), the distribution of sleep onset shifted as a whole (median 23 vs 31 minutes), which indicates a broad effect across participants and not a response confined to a subgroup.
